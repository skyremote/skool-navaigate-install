# Keynote mode

Use only for a spoken stage talk, keynote or TED-style brief. Keep the member's recognisable typography, original
logo and this editable engine. The member's current colour and art-direction requirements override the brand
defaults; do not force a palette into a brief that rejects it.

## Start with the current story

Use the approved talk or current source material. Agree or infer one central idea from that material; every
frame must advance it. A previous deck is a design reference only unless the member asks to reuse its content.
Do not import an old thesis, anecdote or claim because it already has slides.

Map the spoken arc before designing: opening tension, concrete experience, evidence, implication and return. Do
not force an agenda, numbered tips, tool catalogue or sales close into a short talk. Choose the slide count from
the argument and the speaking time; there is no default number of slides per minute.

Set a short art direction from the current brief before building: palette, typography, image treatment, brand
assets and the audience's viewing distance. Record any explicit rejections as constraints. A template or an old
deck cannot silently restore a rejected colour, style or story.

## Design for someone listening

- Give each frame one focal idea with enough visual substance to explain it. A short title is not a completed
  slide. Use an authentic product image, a relevant brand mark, evidence, a diagram or a concrete example where it
  carries the idea. Keep sparse type-only frames (`statement`, `divider`) for deliberate pauses; do not turn the
  whole talk into slogans.
- Keep the full script, delivery cues and detailed citations in the `talk` notes. Keep evidence sources, forecast
  labels and necessary qualifications on the visible slide when their absence would mislead.
- Reuse the builders. Adapt them to larger type and more space; cut or split words before shrinking them. Check
  at presentation size, not only in the contact sheet. Keep roughly five per cent safe margins.
- Vary composition and density within the chosen palette: photographs, meaningful comparisons, process views,
  examples and occasional quiet statements. Inspect the deck as a sequence for repetition.
- Match image composition to the text area. Generate negative space where the words go, keep important subjects
  visible and keep the aspect ratio. Put text on solid ground or a tested backing rather than hoping a photograph
  has enough contrast.
- Use original brand assets. When a talk discusses real products, show their actual marks where they help
  recognition, with their clear space. Label generated scenes and fictional examples in the notes, and visibly
  identify fictional examples when an audience could take them for evidence. Never turn an illustration into a
  quantitative claim or invent a result chart.

## Visual acceptance gate

Before exporting, review the full sequence and then each slide at presentation size:

- Can a viewer recognise the person, product, customer problem or workflow without the speaker notes? If a
  frame is a title plus empty space, add a useful image, comparison, diagram or concrete example, or make it an
  intentional pause in the story.
- Do neighbouring slides vary in composition and density? Repeated slogan cards mean the visual story has not
  been built yet.
- Are logos and photographs authentic and properly sourced? Does generated imagery illustrate an idea without
  pretending to document a real event?
- Are evidence, illustration, fiction and forecast distinguishable where the audience meets them? Is the visible
  text legible at stage size against its actual ground?

## Delivery and proof

Use manual advances unless motion serves the argument. Do not claim a video is embedded unless it is.

Deliver the HTML (for rehearsal: `T` shows the talk track, `S` the speaker view), the editable PPTX, a PDF and
the speaker notes. Keep the spec, the image prompts and the assets with the versioned deck. Inspect every slide,
check the notes survived in the PPTX, spot-check the PDF and open the PPTX in PowerPoint or Keynote when you can,
since fonts and image placement can differ. Report exactly what you tested; a successful render is not a visual
review.

Reference: TED's [in-house slide guidance](https://blog.ted.com/10-tips-for-better-slide-decks/) supports
developing the story first, reducing screen text, using meaningful images and restrained effects. Treat it as
practitioner guidance, not a proven formula.
